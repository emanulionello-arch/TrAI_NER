<doc student="John" title="Too Much Talking, Not Enough Action: My Review of The Picture of Dorian Gray">

When the teacher told us we had to read The Picture of Dorian Gray by Oscar Wilde, I was actually a bit excited at first. The idea of a painting that gets old and ugly while the real person stays young forever sounded like a really cool horror story. But after finishing it, I have to say that I didn't like it very much. In fact, I think it is one of the most boring books we have read this year.
The biggest problem with this novel is that nothing really happens for a long time. The characters just sit around in fancy living rooms drinking tea, smoking, and talking. Lord Henry Wotton is probably the most annoying character ever written. He just talks and talks about youth and beauty in these long monologues that don't make much sense to me. I understand that Oscar Wilde was trying to make a point about Victorian society and how people care too much about how things look, but I felt like I was reading a philosophy textbook instead of a fiction book.
Don't get me wrong, the parts about the painting itself are pretty creepy and interesting. When Dorian looks at the portrait and sees the cruel smile after he breaks Sibyl Vane's heart, that was a very good scene. And the ending is definitely dramatic and bloody. But you have to read through chapters and chapters of dinner parties and boring descriptions of Dorian's weird hobbies (like collecting perfumes and musical instruments) just to get to the good stuff.
If I have to compare it to the other books we read for this English class, I definitely preferred The Strange Case of Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde by Robert Louis Stevenson. Both books are about the dark side of human nature and living a double life. But Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde is a real mystery. There is suspense, a police investigation, and actual action. When Mr. Hyde runs over the little girl or attacks Sir Danvers Carew in the street, you really feel the danger. Dr. Jekyll’s struggle feels more real and scientific, while Dorian Gray just seems like a spoiled rich kid who doesn't want to take responsibility for his bad actions.
In conclusion, I can see why The Picture of Dorian Gray is an important classic. The writing is very elegant, and the original idea of the painting is genius. However, the slow pacing and all the endless philosophical conversations made it very hard for me to enjoy. I think a good story needs a balance of deep ideas and action, and for me, Stevenson did a much better job with that than Wilde.

<doc student="Mary" title="The True Cost of Beauty: Why I Loved The Picture of Dorian Gray">

When our English teacher assigned The Picture of Dorian Gray by Oscar Wilde, I didn't expect to become so obsessed with it. I usually struggle with classic literature because the language can be very old-fashioned, but this book completely captured my attention from the very first chapter. I think it is an absolute masterpiece and definitely my favorite book we have read this semester.
What I loved most about the novel is how psychological it is. The story isn't just about a magical painting; it’s about how toxic relationships can completely ruin your life. Basil Hallward paints this beautiful portrait of his friend, but it is Lord Henry Wotton who really destroys Dorian's soul. Lord Henry is such a fascinating but terrible character. He acts like a mentor, but his constant advice about seeking pleasure and staying young forever acts like a poison. Watching Dorian transform from an innocent young man into a cruel monster who ruins everyone around him in Victorian London was terrifying, but it was impossible to stop reading.
The part that completely broke my heart was the tragic story of Sibyl Vane. She was so talented and innocent, and she loved Dorian so purely. When the book describes her performing as Juliet from Shakespeare's Romeo and Juliet in that cheap, dirty theatre in the East End, you can really feel her passion. The way Dorian treated her just because she had one bad performance was incredibly cruel. That was the moment I realized Dorian was truly evil, even before the terrible crimes he commits later in the story. It actually made me think a lot about our modern society, and how people today still judge others only by their appearance or their success, especially on social media.
Oscar Wilde’s writing style is also incredibly beautiful. Almost every sentence feels like poetry, and I highlighted so many quotes in my copy of the book. The way he describes the dark, foggy streets of London compared to the bright, luxurious mansions creates such a perfect and creepy atmosphere.
In conclusion, I think everyone should read The Picture of Dorian Gray. It is not just an old ghost story; it is a timeless warning about vanity and the danger of caring too much about what is on the outside while ignoring your own soul. I will definitely be reading more plays and novels by Oscar Wilde in the future.

<doc student="Bobby" title="Youth and Consequences in The Picture of Dorian Gray">

Reading The Picture of Dorian Gray by Oscar Wilde was definitely a unique experience for me. It is a very famous novel from the Victorian era, and it deals with many complex themes like aestheticism and hedonism. To be honest, I am not completely sure how I feel about it. It was a very interesting concept, but the story was also quite confusing in some parts and difficult to follow.
The main plot happens in London, where an artist named Basil Hallward paints a beautiful portrait of the main character, Dorian Gray. While Basil is painting, Dorian meets Lord Henry Wotton. Lord Henry is a very influential person who tells Dorian that beauty and youth are the only things that matter in the world. Because of this conversation, Dorian makes a sort of wish that the painting will grow old instead of him. Surprisingly, this actually happens, which is the main supernatural element of the story.
After this, Dorian falls in love with a young actress named Sibyl Vane. However, when she performs badly in a play because she is too in love with him, he rejects her very cruelly. This leads to Sibyl's tragic death. According to the story, this is the exact moment when the portrait changes for the first time, showing a cruel smile. This part was okay, but everything happened very fast and it was hard to understand why Dorian changed his mind about her so quickly.
For the next several years, Dorian lives a secret life of crime and sin in London, but his physical appearance never changes. Only the painting hidden in his attic gets older and uglier, reflecting his corrupted soul. Things get very dark when Basil Hallward tries to confront him, and Dorian ends up murdering Basil to protect his secret. There is also a subplot where Sibyl's brother, James Vane, tries to get revenge on Dorian, but it gets resolved later.
In the final chapter, Dorian decides he wants to be good again, but he realizes he is only doing it for selfish reasons. He takes a knife and stabs the painting. But by doing this, he actually kills himself. The servants find an old, ugly man dead on the floor, and the painting is restored to its original youthful beauty.
In conclusion, I think Oscar Wilde wanted to show the dangers of vanity and living only for pleasure. The ending is very dramatic and proves that you cannot escape your own conscience. However, there were a lot of very long, philosophical conversations that didn't seem necessary to the plot. Overall, it is an important classic book, but I think you really have to focus hard to understand what the author is trying to say.
